1. Opportunity and differentiation
Verdict: a real opportunity, but only if "evidence-backed qualification" is the product
and discovery is treated as a commodity. The PRD is sound on direction. Its main
weakness is breadth: it tries to serve every B2B service provider on day one.

Why it can work

       Lead-data tools (Apollo, Hunter and similar) sell volume and contact data. None of
       what they sell is an explanation of why a company fits this seller. That gap is the PRD's
       thesis, and it is a good one.
       Generic AI cold email is now widely recognised and widely ignored. Evidence-
       grounded, reviewable outreach is a credible counter-position.
       The target users (freelancers, small agencies) cannot afford a research team and are
       underserved by enterprise tools.

Differentiation I would bet on: the outcome loop. Section 18 of the PRD says the platform
"should eventually learn" which signals predict replies. That is the only durable advantage,
and it argues for capturing outcome data (reply, meeting, conversion) from the first
release, even though learning from it is deferred.

Biggest threats: incumbents bolting on AI research (Apollo and others already ship AI
features), and the economics: deep per-company research costs real money per prospect
(see section 6), which constrains pricing for a freelancer audience.

Recommendation: launch the shared platform as specified, but pick one wedge for go-to-
market and prompt tuning (for example, small software and design agencies selling to
other agencies or SaaS companies). Configurability stays; the tested experience is
narrow.


2. Critique of the PRD
The PRD is unusually disciplined about honesty, scope and safety. Its main problems are
an undefined data strategy, a launch audience that is too wide, and several
requirements that cannot be met as written.

Strengths worth keeping

       Evidence taxonomy (verified / source-reported / inferred / unknown) and "missing is
       not positive" scoring rules.
       Human-approved sending as the default; automation deferred.
       Provider adapters, server-side tenant isolation, untrusted-content handling.
       A clear "defer" list that resists copying competitors.

3. Recommended technical architecture
A modular monolith (one repo, one deployable app) on managed PostgreSQL, with a
durable job layer for everything slow, external or billable. The PRD's proposal is broadly
right. I would change three things: add an evidence-verification step that no LLM output
can bypass, keep Gmail to the send-only scope, and avoid microservices entirely at this
stage.

State machines to keep separate
   1. Research state per company: queued, fetching, extracting, scored, failed, stale.
   2. Outreach state per message: draft, approved, queued, sent, bounced, suppressed.
   3. Relationship stage per prospect: the user-visible pipeline.

Mixing these (as the PRD's single stage list does) makes reporting and recovery harder.


What I would not build yet
Microservices, a vector database, a headless-browser fleet, a self-built email verifier,
custom workflow builders.

Relationships
       A workspace has many members, ICPs, campaigns, companies, contacts and email
       accounts.
       A campaign uses one pinned ICP version and holds many prospects
       ( campaign_companies ); a company can appear in several campaigns.
       A company has many evidence records, facts, contacts and research runs; a prospect
       has many assessments, each with eight dimension rows.
       A draft belongs to one prospect and one contact, has many versions, and at most one
       approved version that can produce one email_message .


Integrity rules
   1. Row-level security on every tenant table: a row is visible only if the caller is a member
       of its workspace. Server code also filters by workspace, so a policy mistake alone
       cannot leak data.

   2. Child tables carry workspace_id and use composite foreign keys, so a row in
       workspace A cannot reference a row in workspace B.
   3. Unique keys that make retries safe: company domain per workspace, contact email
       per workspace, idempotency_key on messages, usage events and job runs.
   4. Assessments, draft versions, usage events and activities are append-only.
   5. A send is blocked unless the contact is not suppressed, the draft version is approved,
       and the recipient type is known.

Decision for you: one companies table per workspace (my default) is safest, because no
prospect research crosses tenants. The cost is that two customers researching the same
company pay twice. A later, optional shared cache of purely public facts is possible, but it
creates a privacy question I would not take on in the MVP.

Recommended starting set
   1. Discovery: one search API (Exa first, because it returns page contents and is priced
       per request without a monthly minimum), behind the adapter, plus CSV and URL
       import.
   2. Company facts: the company's own website and team page, fetched by your own
       fetcher. This is the primary evidence source for small firms.
   3. Contacts: public team-page names first; Hunter-style finder and verifier as a
       metered, optional add-on. Offer "bring your own key" for providers whose terms
       prohibit resale, such as Apollo, so the customer's licence covers their own use.
   4. Sending: Gmail with gmail.send only. Start the Google verification process early,
       since it runs on Google's timeline.
   5. Do not scrape LinkedIn or other platforms whose terms prohibit it; the PRD already
       says so.


Not researched (unconfirmed)
       Other search APIs (Tavily, Serper and similar) and other contact databases (People

What moves the number
       Pre-filter before deep research. A cheap homepage classification (a few
       thousandths of a dollar) that rejects obvious non-fits can cut research cost per
       qualified prospect by more than half.

       map to a visible per-company price.

       Pricing implication: at about $26 of variable cost per workspace a month (200
       companies) plus about $15 of fixed cost per workspace at 10 workspaces, a plan under
       roughly $80 to $100 a month leaves little margin once support and payment fees are
       included. That is a hypothesis to test against willingness to pay, not a price
       recommendation.

7. Security and privacy risks
The three risks that can end the business are cross-tenant data exposure, prompt
injection from fetched web pages, and sender-reputation or legal harm caused by one
